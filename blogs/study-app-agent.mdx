The default approach to adding AI to a product is to bolt a chatbot onto the side of an existing product that previously worked without AI. You add a floating widget in the corner, wire up an API endpoint, and suddenly you can call it an ‘AI-powered’ app. The user types questions into a box, and the model generates text; if it's agentic, it might be able to see the current page or search your docs. Sometimes it helps. But I'd argue this interaction design only works when the original app was needlessly complicated; I've never heard someone say “clicking this button has been working fine, but I wish I actually had to write a couple hundred word prompt into a chatbot instead”. Usually what's built is just a worse version of ChatGPT embedded in your interface.

This is the easy path, and so we wanted to test a different idea: what if **the agent is the product**, rather than a feature? What if every core interaction of the study tool (generating study notes, taking quizzes, reviewing flashcards, organising a semester, pacing for an exam) flows through to feed a single agent, and it in turn feeds those features? The idea is to hook the agent loop into the product loop, with a compounding memory store. The user never talks to the agent and never types a prompt. They interact with regular UI elements, such as flashcard decks, calendars, and progress bars, which work better because an agent runs silently behind them.

Over the past while, we have been building out this study application to test that setup. It is a research test bed rather than a polished product, and this post is the overview: the product itself first, then the architecture, the development process, and what happened when we evaluated the system against real benchmarks. Deeper dives on each subsystem will follow in this series.

## The product

Before we talk about the architecture and how we built it, we first should explain what we built. The product is inspired by AI study tools like [StudyFetch](https://www.studyfetch.com/) or [turbo.ai](https://www.turbo.ai/). These tools allow students to upload or capture content from their course, have AI process it and then create study materials like notes, flashcards and quizzes.

The core organisational structure is modules, and these are intended to map to a student's modules on their university course. You can create a module, e.g. "BIO201 — Cell Biology", and can give it a semester and academic year. By default, it files itself under your current semester, which the app can work out from the current date rather than asking. The older semesters and modules stay, just collapsed for easier organisation and to let students focus on their current work.

You can upload content that can either be a document attached to the module in general, like that module's recommended textbook, or as part of a lesson, where it might be the lecture slides or professor's notes. Lessons are essentially just a grouping of documents and recordings associated with a lesson or lecture.

![The modules page, grouped by semester with the current term expanded](/screenshots/study-app-agent/modules.png)

**Uploads.** You drop in whatever content you get on the course - slide decks, Word documents, spreadsheets, PDFs, etc. The app converts them into a viewable format and, if appropriate, renames them to what it is actually about; `619ab3.pdf` means nothing to you, so will be renamed to something sensible. The content is automatically analysed, a concept list and prerequisite graph are generated from the content. This then feeds downstream features. If the content includes new concepts, a flashcard deck gets automatically generated and fed into the global flashcard spaced repetition system.

For each module, we automatically generate and update a study plan to help structure the learning for the student. If they complete a task, we routinely check and can automatically tick off completed items without them needing to remember to update it. As the module/course progresses, the student will record more lectures and upload more content throughout the semester; the system therefore will update the study plan routinely to incorporate these changes.

![Inside a module: the agent-written study plan on top, documents and lessons below](/screenshots/study-app-agent/module-view.png)

![In the module, you can organise lessons and files](/screenshots/study-app-agent/module-files.png)

**Generation.** Every document offers a variety of views on top of it - notes, quizzes, and flashcards, with optional hints to steer their generation. You can also view the concepts that are detected in the document. If you regenerate any of them, we stack versions and allow the student to pick, rather than overwriting them. This allows you to create multiple quizzes or flashcard decks from the same content. Quizzes are classic four-option multiple-choice quizzes; flashcard concepts carry two or three phrasings to encourage the student to learn the underlying concept instead of memorising the specific card.

![A generated quiz on a lecture document, one tab among the document's materials](/screenshots/study-app-agent/document-quiz.png)

**Lectures.** The recording page puts slides on one side and notes on the other; notes you type are timestamped and linked with the audio. You can record with a microphone, the audio of a browser tab playing an online lecture, or both. When you stop, the audio is automatically transcribed and flows through the same pipeline as an upload. The recording, slides, transcript, and notes are grouped as one lecture and can be associated with and organised into a module. This flow allows a student to collect all the content from lectures over a semester and then have appropriate study materials ambiently served to them, and their learning tracked to inform future generated material.

![The lecture recording page: slide preview, timestamped notes, source picker](/screenshots/study-app-agent/record.png)

**Studying.** Concepts you are about to forget, ranked by predicted recall, mixed with new material at a ratio that keeps the student in an ideal level of **desirable difficulty** with approximately 70–85% accuracy. This helps learning stick as students are getting positive affirmation and encouragement and stay engaged. Getting 80% right gives them the required dopamine hits; however, that 20% they get wrong will stretch them, ensuring they aren’t wasting their time and not actually learning. Every answer updates a per-concept competency level and a spaced-repetition schedule, so the next session is built from everything before it.

![A composed study session — review and new cards, flip to answer, self-grade](/screenshots/study-app-agent/study.png)

**The visible model.** We surface a dashboard with a list of all the concepts, recall probabilities, with filters for weak/due/mastered concepts. Each concept opens a view showing all the documents, quiz questions, and flashcards the concept is mentioned in. The profile card lets users see how the agent sees them: a narrative profile the agent wrote about them based on their behaviour - when you study, how fast you answer, what you abandon, what you ace and what you struggle with. This profile and data are used to tailor all the generated content to you.

![The concepts dashboard: predicted recall per concept, due and weak filters](/screenshots/study-app-agent/concepts.png)

!["How the agent sees you": the agent's narrative profile, written from the behaviour ledger](/screenshots/study-app-agent/mirror.png)

!["Chart and statistics of what the user studies and where they are struggling, this is used as knowledge for the agent](/screenshots/study-app-agent/mirror-data.png)

**What to do next.** The home page recommends what is likely to be the next focus for the user. Each suggestion includes a reference (e.g. "You recall Chlorophyll slowly, ~34s avg — a targeted quiz would tighten that"), and carries today's item from the relevant study plans. We collect the exam dates for the study plans to pace the plan toward the exam, and the plan automatically updates as new content arrives. Taking the suggested quiz checks off the plan item by itself. When you upload content, we prompt you to organise content eagerly and prevent you from becoming disorganised. You can manually move content between modules in one step.

![The home page: recommendations that cite their evidence and today's plan item](/screenshots/study-app-agent/home.png)

We also have a dark mode for the app.
![The concepts dashboard in dark mode](/screenshots/study-app-agent/concepts-dark.png)

None of this is a chat interface. There is no prompt box anywhere. The agent's work (scheduling, generating, ranking, narrating) surfaces as ordinary UI. A user may never know it is a unified AI agent as the engine of the product. All this could have been achieved with isolated features, but as we add features and they impact each other, it would become a spaghetti-like mess. We also wanted the product to learn and grow with the user, personalising all the features and parts to them, understanding the student, how they learn and what they struggle with. We believe the best form of education is a great human tutor who can tailor the education to you; this is trying to replicate some of what makes that work so well.

## The agent spectrum

To see why this app works the way it does, it helps to look at how most developer-facing AI agents operate, as these are currently at the forefront of agent design. The standard design for an AI agent today is the [ReAct](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629) (Reason + Act) tool-calling loop, used in tools like [Claude Code](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-code) and [Cursor](https://cursor.com).

In an interactive coding tool, the loop looks like this — step through it, or press play:

<ReActLoop client:visible />

This loop works well for command-line and code-editing tools because they have the following properties:

1. **Explicit goals:** The user specifies a clear task in natural language, like "Fix the broken auth route."
2. **Clear feedback:** The environment gives immediate feedback to the agent, like results from running unit tests or failed compiles.
3. **Short sessions:** The agent runs in an isolated, context-free session until the work finishes; it doesn’t need a global memory or shared understanding of the user.

### Why this doesn’t normally work in traditional apps

In most consumer software, including our learning app, those three assumptions don’t hold:

- **Students shouldn't have to prompt.** A student just wants to open the app, click a few buttons and review their content; they shouldn’t have to write prompts for basic interactions with the product.
- **The environment isn't deterministic.** You can't run a unit test on humans' memory to verify if someone remembers a concept. The agent has to handle uncertainty around memory decay, focus, and answering speed. The environment of human learning is just more complicated and complex than a code repo.
- **Memory must last across sessions.** A tool-calling loop relies on context-free chat history; this works fine for an AI coding session. A study app needs a persistent memory store that keeps track of what we learn and know about the student, survives across semesters and tracks concept decay and study habits over months or years.

Instead of an interactive terminal agent that waits for prompts, this app uses an **ambient agent**. It sits behind regular UI elements, receives events, updates a persistent user model, and presents relevant study material.

## The shared backbone

Instead of creating separate isolated features with their own prompts, context and API endpoints, every feature runs through a single agent pipeline:

```
analyze_document → plan → retrieve_memory → generate → validate → finalize
```

Our agent utilises [LangGraph](https://langchain-ai.github.io/langgraph/) with multiple nodes in the graph and tools the agent can call and utilise. The agent reads the document, plans output parameters, fetches memory about the user, generates content, validates the structure, and saves the result. We utilise a **task_type** parameter that picks which tool runs during generation and therefore which feature, e.g. flashcards, quiz, notes summary, etc.

### The memory architecture

We want our agent to personalise for the user, and that requires remembering and understanding them; therefore, we need a unified memory. To unify the memory, we store it all together; document analyses, concept mastery tallies, [FSRS](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki/wiki/The-Algorithm) schedules, preferences, and prerequisite graphs all use this table. The memory operates at multiple granularities, from the document to the user level.

Keeping the store simple means new signals can drop straight in easily. The retrieve_memory node in the LangGraph agent picks them up on the next generation, and as all the features contribute to this memory, the agent has always-up-to-date information.

## System architecture

Instead of building separate microservices or isolated LLM handlers for each tool, we organise the application features into the following core layers centred around a unified memory store and an **event bus**.

<SystemArchitecture client:visible />

The system components divide into five primary functional responsibilities:

- **Ingestion and boundary handling:** We convert incoming files (`.docx`, `.pptx`, raw audio recordings) into standardised PDFs for the user and plain markdown text for the AI agent to utilise, before we run the internal processing and data extraction.
- **Shared agent backbone:** We execute generation tasks through a single LangGraph agent pipeline using a generic key-value memory store (agent_memory).
- **Asynchronous event bus:** This decouples the user interactions from background side effects, running tasks like concept graph updates, review deck creation, and profile updates independently. It allows any feature to get notified when an action has taken place, without us needing to explicitly trigger the feature.
- **Telemetry and recommendation engine:** Captures client-side metrics like reading dwell time and answer latency to score and suggest study priorities.
- **Evaluation harness:** Runs pytest suites with [DeepEval](https://github.com/confident-ai/deepeval) against public datasets ([SciQ](https://allenai.org/data/sciq), [RACE](https://huggingface.co/datasets/ehovy/race), [Duolingo](https://github.com/duolingo/halflife-regression), [EdNet](https://github.com/riiid/ednet)) using a stronger LLM judge model to measure generation quality and memory calibration.

## Key architectural deep dives

### The event-driven architecture

As we added more features and background features, and they in turn had side effects, our application grew unwieldy. An example of this is that once a recording is added, we need to automatically transcribe it; once a text document is added (either from transcription or a user uploading a document), we then need to run concept extraction. Hopefully this illustrates how the complexity can grow, and if each feature needs to explicitly trigger the next action, they accumulate too many responsibilities. A single quiz submission handler grew significantly; it had to handle synchronously updating concept mastery, calculating FSRS schedules, updating user profiles, checking weak topics, and writing telemetry logs. If a secondary step raised an error, the main quiz submission would either have to try again, rerunning all the successfully completed actions or the background errors could be ignored, but they'd then vanish inside the main quiz submission task. Neither of these is ideal.

Our solution was to utilise an event-driven approach. This works with a publish-and-subscribe philosophy, so when any action happens (like a quiz being submitted), any other feature can listen for that event and trigger its own action accordingly (e.g. the FSRS schedule can be updated). This elegantly compartmentalises the architecture and allows it to be more easily extended; for example, if the FSRS schedule updater wants to listen for completed flashcards in addition to quizzes, it can simply also subscribe to flashcards completed events. Our event bus fixed this by separating database commits from background side effects:

```py
# 1. Primary write commits synchronously
session.add(quiz_attempt)
await session.commit()




# 2. Side effects publish asynchronously
await bus.publish(QuizAttempted(
user_id=user.id,
quiz_id=quiz.id,
answers=payload.answers,
latency_ms=payload.latency_ms
))
```

Subscribed handlers execute independently, wrapping their logic in isolated database sessions:

```py
@bus.subscribe(QuizAttempted)
async def update_concept_mastery_handler(event: QuizAttempted):
async with db.session_factory() as session:
# Calculate FSRS update & concept tallies
...
await session.commit()
```

The system records every dispatch and result in an append-only **agent_events** ledger, so we have a full history of actions:

| event_id  | event_type    | handler                | status | error           | timestamp            |
| :-------- | :------------ | :--------------------- | :----- | :-------------- | :------------------- |
| evt_10492 | QuizAttempted | update_concept_mastery | OK     | NULL            | 2026-08-18T08:12:01Z |
| evt_10493 | QuizAttempted | trigger_proactive_deck | FAILED | LLMTimeoutError | 2026-08-18T08:12:02Z |

### Passive telemetry and latency profiling

Quizzes provide direct but infrequent data. Passive telemetry fills the gaps by recording client interaction details.
The client collects events (document.opened, question.answered, session.abandoned) and sends them via [navigator.sendBeacon](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Navigator/sendBeacon) when the user leaves or hits a batch threshold.

<TelemetryPipeline client:visible />

Answering latency adds context when merged into FSRS concept entries:

$$
\text{Mastery State} = f(\text{Accuracy}, \text{Latency}, \text{FSRS Stability})
$$

- **Fast + Correct:** Solid mastery.
- **Slow + Correct:** Fragile mastery; needs spaced review.
- **Fast + Incorrect:** Misconception; needs explanation.
- **Slow + Incorrect:** Knowledge gap; needs foundational review.

### Adaptive study plan engine

Static study plans break as soon as new materials are uploaded. The app uses an adaptive planning loop that bases schedules on current course state while limiting token costs through throttling.

#### Updates and cooldowns

When events mark a plan as outdated (e.g. a Document is Ingested, Quiz Attempted, etc), the system logs a staleness reason. To keep LLM usage reasonable, plan regenerations enforce a strict **one-update-per-module-per-day** cooldown, to prevent too many study plan regenerations.

```py
if plan.is_stale and (now - plan.last_regenerated_at) > timedelta(hours=24):
await regenerate_plan_task(module_id)
```

## Next steps

Our intention for building this was twofold: first, we wanted to explore these ideas around AI agent-driven products and ideas around EdTech. But we also wanted a realistic app that can act as a test bed for future experiments and research, allowing us to plug in and apply much of our current exploration into a real app. We're planning many more future posts building out various elements of our prototype study app. Some of the things we are currently working on and researching:

- **Custom embeddings solutions** and runtimes to allow for fast, cheap, scalable and high-quality information processing, grouping and recall/searching. We'll also explore more interesting ways to integrate this with agents beyond simple RAG, with agentic searching and tool use
- **Self-hosted transcription models** for significantly faster and cheaper transcription. Plus ways we can measure ASR quality and boost accuracy, especially in challenging audio situations
- **Knowledge graph extraction without LLMs**, allowing us to have fast, cheap and scalable concept and relationship extraction
- **Agent sandboxes and runtimes**, exploring ideas around neuro-symbolic AI and improving AI quality by integrating tooling like algebra and maths solvers or physics simulators
- **Automatically aligning slides** in a presentation to audio based on the transcript, allowing for grouping content based on the slides and associating a slide with a transcript section for deeper context
- **Tutor bots** and concepts around authentic practice, and bringing AI tutoring and learning into a student's existing workflow and life. It's inspired by [Andy Matuschak’s](https://andymatuschak.org/) brilliant [“How we might learn”](https://andymatuschak.org/hmwl/)
- **OCR, document processing and accessibility** research, including PDF accessibility remediation, with open-source tooling like [Docling](https://github.com/docling-project/docling). Self-hosted and managed for lower cost and scalable document handling
- **Fine-tuning small models** for extremely fast summarisation and wiki-fication. Combining with the knowledge graph extraction, this lets us build evergreen AI-generated wikis about any concept, constantly up to date based on the content the students have captured. The fine-tuning is important to allow us to use smaller models and to get human-sounding writing rather than ‘AI slop’ text
- **Deep dive into quiz generation**, quality and evaluations. Fine-tuning small models to generate ideal quizzes and looking into automatic short-answer grading research for different quiz modalities
- **Further research on recommendation systems** and behavioural modelling to better recommend actions and schedule work for the student, based on their behaviour. Building a notification system to encourage students to engage with their learning and content, gamifying it, similar to Duolingo

As you can see, there is a lot we are working on in this space, and this series will continue long-term, whilst also publishing other content! In the meantime, we are planning to publish more blog posts that go deeper into how and why we built our current prototype as we did.
